Why Do Seventh-day Adventists Worship on Saturday?

By Jack Thompson · Oct 25, 2026 · 16 min read

The Bible story, the history, and the heart behind Sabbath worship, plus honest answers to the questions people ask most.

Maybe a coworker turned down your Saturday barbecue because she had church that morning. Maybe you drove past a full parking lot on a Saturday and wondered what was going on inside. Or maybe you grew up keeping Sunday, and one day someone handed you a Bible verse that left you more curious than you expected. Questions like these are how many people first meet Seventh-day Adventists, and they are good questions to ask.

Whatever brought you here, it deserves more than a one line answer. Seventh-day Adventists worship on Saturday because the Bible names the seventh day as the Sabbath that God blessed at Creation, wrote into the Ten Commandments, and that Jesus Himself kept. For Adventists, this is not a rule for earning salvation or a badge of being better than other Christians. It is a weekly appointment with the God who has already saved them, a standing invitation to stop, rest, and remember who they belong to.

In this article we will walk slowly through what Scripture says from Genesis to Revelation, look honestly at how Sunday worship became common in church history, answer the passages people most often raise, and paint a picture of what the Sabbath actually looks like in a real home. You do not have to agree with everything here to find it helpful. Our hope is simply that you will open your own Bible, read the passages for yourself, and let God speak.

Adventists keep Saturday as the Sabbath because the Bible consistently calls the seventh day holy, and nowhere in Scripture does God move that blessing to another day. From the first chapter of Genesis to the prophecies of the new earth, the seventh day stands out as a day God made, blessed, and set apart for Himself and for His people.

The Sabbath runs from Friday at sunset to Saturday at sunset. That may sound unusual today, but it follows the biblical way of counting days from evening to evening. In the Creation story each day is described as "the evening and the morning" (Genesis 1:5), God later told Israel to keep holy time "from even unto even" (Leviticus 23:32), and the Gospels show people waiting until the sun had set on the Sabbath before bringing their sick to Jesus (Mark 1:32). So when the sun goes down on Friday, holy time begins.

That conviction rests on five threads that run through the whole Bible. Each one strengthens the others, and together they form the reason Adventists gather for worship on the seventh day.

The five threads at a glance

1Creation: God blessed the seventh day before sin or any nation existed (Genesis 2:2 to 3)

2Commandment: the fourth of the Ten Commandments names the seventh day (Exodus 20:8 to 11)

3Christ: Jesus kept the Sabbath and called Himself its Lord (Luke 4:16, Mark 2:28)

4Church: the apostles kept worshiping on the Sabbath after the resurrection (Acts 13, 16, 17, 18)

5Covenant: the Sabbath is a sign of freedom and belonging to God (Ezekiel 20:20)

1. The Sabbath began at Creation, before there were any Jews

The Sabbath is older than Israel, older than Moses, and older than sin itself. It did not begin at Mount Sinai or in a synagogue. It began in the Garden of Eden, in a perfect world, as the final touch on God's work of Creation.

"And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made. And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it." Genesis 2:2 to 3, KJV

Notice that God did three things to that day. He rested on it, He blessed it, and He sanctified it, which means He set it apart for holy use. He did not do these things to any other day of the week. God was not tired, so His rest was not about exhaustion. It was about delight in what He had made and about creating space for relationship with the people He had just formed in His image.

This matters because many sincere Christians assume the Sabbath was given only to the Jewish nation. Yet Jesus said plainly, "The sabbath was made for man" (Mark 2:27). The word He used points to humanity as a whole, not to one ethnic group. Adam and Eve were not Jewish. Abraham would not be born for many generations. The Sabbath was a gift to the entire human family, woven into the rhythm of the week before any nation existed.

The Seventh-day Adventist Church sets out this teaching in Fundamental Belief 20, which describes the Sabbath as a memorial of Creation. That is a beautiful way to think about it. In a world that often tells us we are accidents, every Saturday becomes a weekly reminder that we did not make ourselves. Someone made us on purpose, loves us deeply, and invites us to rest in His care.

2. God placed the Sabbath in the heart of the Ten Commandments

Centuries later, when God rescued Israel from slavery in Egypt, He gathered them at Mount Sinai and spoke the Ten Commandments aloud. Then He wrote them on tablets of stone with His own finger, something He did with no other part of the law. Right in the middle of those ten, the fourth commandment named a specific day.

"Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy. Six days shalt thou labour, and do all thy work: But the seventh day is the sabbath of the LORD thy God." Exodus 20:8 to 10, KJV

Look closely at the very first word: remember. God was not introducing something new at Sinai. He was calling His people back to something He had already established, something their long years of slavery may have caused them to forget. The commandment even explains why in verse 11, pointing straight back to Creation, when the Lord made heaven and earth in six days and rested on the seventh.

Most Christians agree that the other nine commandments still guide us today. We still believe that lying, stealing, murder, adultery, and idolatry are wrong, and we teach our children to honor their parents. Adventists simply ask a gentle question: why would the one commandment that begins with the word "remember" be the one we are free to forget? Jesus connected love and obedience when He said, "If ye love me, keep my commandments" (John 14:15). Obedience is not how we earn His love. It is how we respond to it.

3. Jesus kept the Sabbath and called Himself its Lord

If anyone had the authority to set the Sabbath aside, it was Jesus. Yet the Gospels show Him keeping it faithfully, week after week, throughout His ministry. He never treated the Sabbath as something to throw away. He treated it as something to rescue from the burdens people had placed on it.

Luke tells us that when Jesus came to Nazareth, He went into the synagogue on the Sabbath day "as his custom was" (Luke 4:16). Sabbath worship was His regular habit, not an occasional visit. The Son of God, who had been present at Creation, spent His seventh days among God's people, reading Scripture and teaching.

At the same time, Jesus pushed back hard against the heavy human rules that religious leaders had piled onto the day. Some had turned the Sabbath into a list of restrictions so detailed that it felt more like a burden than a blessing. Jesus healed on the Sabbath, allowed His hungry disciples to pick grain, and taught that "it is lawful to do well on the sabbath days" (Matthew 12:12). He was not abolishing the Sabbath. He was restoring it to what God always intended: a day of healing, mercy, freedom, and joy.

He also called Himself "Lord also of the sabbath" (Mark 2:28). That title carries real weight. If Jesus is Lord of the Sabbath, then the Sabbath is, in a very real sense, the Lord's Day. Keeping it is a way of honoring Him as the Creator who made us and the Redeemer who restores us.

Even at the end of His earthly life, His followers honored the day. After the crucifixion, the women who loved Him prepared spices but "rested the sabbath day according to the commandment" (Luke 23:56). Then they came to the tomb early on the first day of the week (Luke 24:1). Scripture marks three days in a row here: the preparation day when Jesus died, the Sabbath when He rested in the tomb, and the first day when He rose. Even in death, Jesus rested on the seventh day, just as He had at Creation.

4. The apostles kept worshiping on the Sabbath after the resurrection

If the resurrection had moved worship from Saturday to Sunday, we would expect the book of Acts to tell us so. Acts records the life of the early church for roughly three decades after Jesus rose, and it is full of details about where and when the believers gathered. What we actually find is the apostles meeting on the Sabbath, year after year, in city after city.

When Paul and Barnabas arrived in Antioch of Pisidia, they went to the synagogue on the Sabbath and preached Christ. The Gentiles who heard them asked to hear more "the next sabbath," and on that following Sabbath nearly the whole city came together to hear the word of God (Acts 13:14, 42, 44). If Sunday had already become the Christian day of worship, this would have been the perfect moment to invite those Gentiles back the very next morning. Instead, they waited a full week for the Sabbath.

The pattern continues throughout Paul's travels. In Philippi, where there was no synagogue at all, Paul and his companions went out on the Sabbath to a riverside place where people gathered for prayer, and there Lydia believed (Acts 16:13). In Thessalonica, Paul reasoned with the people from the Scriptures for three Sabbaths, "as his manner was" (Acts 17:2), the same phrase Luke used about Jesus. In Corinth, he taught every Sabbath for a year and a half, persuading both Jews and Greeks (Acts 18:4, 11).

The New Testament also looks forward rather than backward. Hebrews 4:9 says, "There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God." The Greek word used there is sabbatismos, which literally means a Sabbath keeping. And the prophet Isaiah pictures the redeemed in the new earth coming to worship God "from one sabbath to another" (Isaiah 66:22 to 23). The Sabbath began in Eden, and Scripture shows it continuing into eternity.

5. The Sabbath is a sign of freedom and belonging

When Moses repeated the Ten Commandments to a new generation in Deuteronomy, he gave a second reason to keep the Sabbath. Alongside Creation, he pointed to redemption: "remember that thou wast a servant in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 5:15). Slaves in Egypt never got a day off. Free people do. The Sabbath was a weekly celebration that God had broken their chains.

For Christians, that deliverance points straight to Jesus, who frees us from the slavery of sin and fear. God calls the Sabbath a sign "that ye may know that I am the LORD your God" (Ezekiel 20:20). Think of it like a wedding ring. Wearing a ring does not make you married, and taking it off does not end the marriage. But the ring is a visible, daily sign of a relationship and a promise. The Sabbath works the same way between God and His people.

This is exactly why Adventists insist that Sabbath keeping is never about earning salvation. We are saved by grace through faith in Jesus alone, and not by our own works (Ephesians 2:8 to 9). In fact, resting on the Sabbath is one of the clearest pictures of grace there is. Once a week we stop working, stop striving, and stop trying to prove ourselves. We trust God to hold our lives together while we rest, which is what faith looks like in practice.

So how did most Christians end up worshiping on Sunday?

If the Bible is this consistent about the seventh day, it is natural to ask how most of the Christian world came to worship on Sunday. The honest answer is that the shift came gradually over several centuries, through church tradition and civil law, rather than through a command in Scripture. Many historians, including Catholic and Protestant scholars, describe Sunday observance as a development of the centuries after the apostles.

In the second century, some Christians began gathering on Sunday to honor the resurrection. Many historians link the growing preference for Sunday to a desire to separate the church from Judaism during a time when Jews faced suspicion and hostility from the Roman empire. Over time, what began as an additional gathering in some places slowly replaced the Sabbath in others.

Civil law then reinforced the change. On March 7, 321, Emperor Constantine issued the first known civil law calling for rest on "the venerable day of the sun," with an exception allowing farmers to keep working. Later in the fourth century, the regional Council of Laodicea instructed Christians to work on Saturday and to honor Sunday instead. Notice that the council needed to say this at all, which shows that many believers were still keeping the seventh day.

Throughout those centuries and beyond, faithful groups of believers in many lands continued to keep the Sabbath, and the practice never fully disappeared. In the 1840s, the founders of the Adventist movement learned the seventh day Sabbath from Seventh Day Baptists, studied the Scriptures carefully, and became convinced it was a truth worth restoring for the church in the last days.

It is important to say this clearly: Adventists do not believe that Christians who worship on Sunday are insincere or less loved by God. Millions of faithful believers worship on Sunday with all their hearts, and God sees every one of them. The question Adventists raise is not about anyone's sincerity. It is simply this: what does Scripture itself say, and will we follow it wherever it leads?

Is Saturday really the seventh day?

Yes, and this is easier to confirm than many people expect. The weekly cycle has never been broken. When the Gregorian calendar replaced the older Julian calendar in 1582, ten dates were dropped to correct the calendar's drift, but the days of the week stayed in perfect order. Thursday, October 4 was followed by Friday, October 15. The dates changed, but the week did not.

The Jewish people have also kept the seventh day continuously since ancient times, which provides a living witness to which day the Sabbath falls on. Jesus kept the same weekly Sabbath as the Jewish community of His day, and that same day continues to come around every Saturday.

Language tells the story too. In many languages around the world, the name for Saturday comes directly from the word Sabbath. Spanish says sábado, Italian says sabato, and French says samedi. In Haitian Creole, Saturday is samdi, a word that traces back through French and Latin to the Sabbath. Many Haitian families have been naming the Sabbath every single week without realizing it.

Honest questions people ask

Whenever this topic comes up, a few Bible passages are usually raised as evidence that the Sabbath has changed. These are fair questions, and they deserve careful answers rather than quick dismissals. Here is how Adventists understand each one.

PassageWhat it describesWhat it does not say
Acts 20:7A farewell meeting, likely Saturday night, before Paul leftThat the first day became holy
1 Corinthians 16:2Setting aside relief money at homeThat a worship service was held
Colossians 2:16Yearly ceremonial feasts that pointed to ChristThat the Creation Sabbath ended
Romans 14:5Disputes over food and fast daysAnything about the Sabbath
Revelation 1:10John in the Spirit on the Lord's dayThat the Lord's day is Sunday

Does Acts 20:7 show the early church met on Sunday?

Acts 20:7 describes a farewell meeting in the city of Troas on the first day of the week, when Paul preached until midnight because he was leaving the next morning. In biblical reckoning, the first day of the week began at sunset on Saturday, so this was most likely a Saturday night gathering, and Paul set out on his journey on Sunday morning. The passage also mentions breaking bread, which the believers did daily according to Acts 2:46. Acts 20:7 faithfully records a special event, but it never commands a new day of worship or calls the first day holy.

What about 1 Corinthians 16:2?

In this verse Paul asked believers to set money aside "by him in store" on the first day of the week, as an offering for famine relief for the struggling believers in Jerusalem. The wording points to each person setting funds aside at home before the week's spending began, so that the gift would be ready when Paul arrived and no collection would be needed then. It is a practical plan for gathering relief offerings, and it does not describe a worship service or mention any change to the Sabbath.

Doesn't Colossians 2:16 say the Sabbath no longer matters?

Colossians 2:16 to 17 mentions "holy days," "new moon," and "sabbath days," and calls them a shadow of things to come. Adventists understand this list as the yearly ceremonial feasts of Israel and their special sabbaths, which were tied to the sacrificial system and pointed forward to Christ's sacrifice. The same grouping of feasts, new moons, and sabbaths appears in Old Testament passages about temple offerings, such as Ezekiel 45:17. The weekly Sabbath is different, because it points back to Creation rather than forward to the cross, and something that began in a sinless Eden could not be a shadow of a remedy for sin.

Doesn't Romans 14:5 say every day is alike?

Romans 14 deals with disputes among believers over food and over certain days connected with fasting and feasting. The word Sabbath does not appear anywhere in the chapter. Paul's point is that Christians should stop judging one another over personal customs and matters of conscience. Reading the chapter as a quiet cancellation of one of the Ten Commandments would ask far more of it than Paul ever intended.

Isn't Sunday the Lord's Day because of the resurrection?

The resurrection is the heart of the gospel, and Adventists celebrate it with great joy. Without it, our faith would be empty. Yet the New Testament gives baptism, not a new weekly day, as the memorial of the death, burial, and resurrection of Jesus (Romans 6:4). When John writes that he was in the Spirit on "the Lord's day" (Revelation 1:10), the rest of Scripture helps us identify which day the Lord calls His own. God calls the Sabbath "my holy day" (Isaiah 58:13), and Jesus calls Himself Lord of the Sabbath (Mark 2:28).

What Sabbath actually looks like

Many people imagine the Sabbath as a long list of things you are not allowed to do. In practice, for most Adventist families, it feels much more like a weekly holiday with God and the people you love. It is the day the whole week leans toward.

STEP 1FridayPrepare the home and meals
STEP 2SunsetFamily worship begins the Sabbath
STEP 3MorningSabbath School and worship
STEP 4AfternoonNature, service, fellowship
STEP 5Saturday sunsetClose with prayer

Isaiah invites us to "call the sabbath a delight" (Isaiah 58:13), and that word captures the spirit of it well. For many families, Friday becomes a day of preparation, with the house tidied, the meals cooked ahead, and the errands finished. As the sun sets, the family gathers for a simple worship with songs, a Bible reading, and prayer. Phones and screens are set aside, and a warm meal is shared without the usual rush.

Saturday morning brings Sabbath School, where people of every age study the Bible together in small classes, followed by the main worship service with music, prayer, and preaching. The afternoon is open time with God. Families might take a walk in nature, visit someone who is sick or lonely, read together, or simply enjoy long conversations around the table. Work, shopping, and the usual chase of the week pause until sunset, and the quiet itself becomes a gift.

For many Haitian families, the Sabbath table is where faith, culture, and community meet, and where the joy of the day is easiest to feel.

Reflection questions

Take a few quiet minutes with these questions, either on your own or with someone you trust. First, ask yourself what would change in your week if you set aside 24 hours to rest in God without any guilt. Then consider whether your view of the Sabbath has come from what Scripture actually says, or mostly from what you have always heard. Finally, think about how a weekly day of rest might remind you that your worth comes from God's love rather than from how much you get done.

A word of encouragement

If this is all new to you, there is no need to settle every question today. God is patient with honest seekers, and He meets each of us where we are. The Sabbath is not a burden He places on tired shoulders. It is an invitation from a Savior who said, "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28).

Open your Bible, read these passages for yourself, and ask Him to guide you. Talk with Him about what you find. The same God who rested on the seventh day at Creation still longs to spend time with you, and He has set aside a whole day every week for exactly that.
